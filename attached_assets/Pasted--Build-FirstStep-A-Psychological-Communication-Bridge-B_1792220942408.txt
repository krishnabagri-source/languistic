# Build FirstStep — A Psychological Communication Bridge

Build a polished, responsive web application called **FirstStep** with the tagline:

**"Bridge the gap between what you feel and what you want to say."**

## 1. Core purpose

FirstStep is a psychological communication-support tool designed to help people overcome internal barriers that make communication difficult.

These barriers include:
- Fear of being judged or misunderstood.
- Fear of rejection or confrontation.
- Overthinking the meaning of another person's message.
- Difficulty expressing emotions clearly.
- Hesitation to ask questions or seek help.
- Difficulty saying no or setting boundaries.
- Anxiety about approaching friends, seniors, teachers, teammates, or colleagues.

The application should help a user move from confusion, hesitation, or emotional overwhelm toward clearer understanding and more confident communication.

This is NOT a generic chatbot, a therapy application, or a mental-health diagnosis tool.

**The main workflow is: Receive a message → Understand possible meanings → Reflect on the psychological barrier → Decide what to communicate → Prepare a response in the user's own voice.**

The website must emphasize empathy, emotional safety, accessibility, and human control.

## 2. Technology requirements

Use:
- React
- Vite
- TypeScript
- Tailwind CSS
- Lucide React icons

Build a frontend-first prototype that runs without a backend, database, or paid API key.

Implement a small amount of real functionality using local TypeScript logic, predefined examples, and deterministic mock analysis.

Do not pretend that a real AI model is connected. Clearly label results as demonstration analysis.

Structure the code so a real AI backend can be integrated later without rewriting the whole application.

Keep components, types, example scenarios, and analysis logic in separate files.

6. Difficulty setting boundaries.

### How it works

Display four steps:

1. Share the message and optional situation.
2. Explore what the message says and what it might mean.
3. Understand your communication goal and what is making the conversation difficult.
4. Prepare a response that feels authentic to you.

### Our principles

Create four polished cards:

**Understanding**
Separate what was actually said from possible interpretations. Acknowledge uncertainty and never claim to know another person's thoughts.

**Emotional Safety**
Offer nonjudgmental guidance, let users pause or leave, and never pressure them to reply, apologize, forgive, or reconcile.

**Accessibility**
Use readable text, good contrast, keyboard navigation, visible focus indicators, accessible form labels, and responsive layouts.

**Human Control**
Users can edit, reject, regenerate, or copy suggestions. Nothing is automatically sent. The app supports decision-making rather than making decisions for the user.

## 6. Implement working demonstration analysis

After submission, display a brief loading animation and generate a structured result using local TypeScript logic.

The analysis must change based on the message example, situation, selected psychological barriers, user's goal, and preferred tone.

Use a simple, honest mock-analysis engine. Create specific result templates for the supplied examples and a neutral fallback for unknown messages.

Do not simply display the same generic result for every input.

Clearly label the output:

"Prototype analysis — illustrative suggestions, not verified AI interpretation."

### Result 1: What was actually said?

Summarize the literal message in neutral language.

Clearly distinguish the sender's actual words from interpretations of those words.

### Result 2: What could it mean?

Offer two or three plausible interpretations when appropriate.

For each interpretation, include:
- Possible meaning.
- What in the message supports that possibility.
- What remains unknown.

Use wording such as:
- "One possibility is..."
- "Depending on the context..."
- "This message alone does not tell us..."
- "You could ask for clarification if you are unsure."

Never declare that the sender is angry, manipulative, toxic, jealous, or intentionally hurtful without sufficient evidence.

Do not imply that the application can read minds or accurately infer hidden intentions.

### Result 3: Understand your barrier

Reflect the barrier selected by the user.

For example, if they selected fear of judgment, display:

"Fear of being judged can make it difficult to say what you really mean. You do not have to find perfect words before you begin."

If they selected overthinking, display:

"When a message feels unclear, your mind may generate several explanations. Try separating what you know from what you are guessing."

If they selected fear of confrontation, display:

"You can be clear about your feelings without making the conversation hostile. It is also okay to pause before responding."

If the user did not select a barrier, do not assign one to them.

Include one short reflection exercise relevant to the selected barrier.

### Result 4: Separate facts from assumptions

Create an interactive card with two columns:

"What I know"
- Information directly supported by the message or context.

"What I am guessing"
- Possible interpretations that have not been confirmed.

For a generic message, avoid inventing facts. Let the user edit both columns.

Include a reflection question:

"What else could explain this message?"

### Result 5: What do you want them to understand?

Reflect the user's stated communication goal and the context they supplied.

Show the user's original explanation in an editable field.

If they did not specify what they want to convey, invite them to add it without making it mandatory.

### Result 6: Prepare your response

Generate three locally defined response examples based on the selected goal and tone.

Include:
1. Gentle and empathetic.
2. Clear and direct.
3. Firm but respectful, when appropriate.

Examples should be natural and concise, not robotic.

For a message such as "We need to talk", example responses might include:
- Warm: "Of course. Is there something specific you'd like us to discuss?"
- Neutral: "Sure. When would be a good time?"
- Direct: "Okay. Can you give me a little context so I know what to expect?"

These are examples, not universal answers.

Every response must be editable in a text area.

Include functional buttons:
- Copy response.
- Try another version.
- Edit response.

When the user changes the selected tone, update the suggested responses.

The regenerate button must actually cycle through alternative local templates rather than repeating the same response.

### Result 7: Before you send anything

Create a supportive reflection card with three optional questions:

- "Am I responding to what they actually said, or to what I fear they meant?"
- "What do I want this person to understand?"
- "Does this response sound like me?"

Add a final choice:
- I feel ready to respond.
- I want to edit my response.
- I would rather wait.
- I need more clarity first.

Do not make the user feel that replying immediately is necessary.

## 7. Emotional safety requirements

Emotional safety must be a core product behavior.

- Use respectful, nonjudgmental language.
- Validate the user's feelings without automatically agreeing with every interpretation.
- Do not pressure users into forgiveness, reconciliation, confrontation, or disclosure.
- Allow users to choose not to reply.
- Do not diagnose psychological conditions.
- Do not claim to treat anxiety, depression, trauma, or other conditions.
- Do not encourage the user to send a response that could put them at risk.
- If a scenario indicates threats, coercion, or immediate danger, prioritize the user's safety and trusted human support instead of recommending confrontation.
- Avoid manipulative scripts, guilt-tripping, threats, or emotionally coercive language.

The product should help people think more clearly without pretending to replace a trusted person or qualified professional.

## 8. Accessibility and privacy

Accessibility:
- Use semantic HTML and properly associated labels.
- Ensure keyboard navigation works.
- Add visible focus states.
- Maintain readable contrast and font sizes.
- Support mobile, tablet, and desktop layouts.
- Do not use color as the only signal.
- Support reduced-motion preferences.
- Make all dialogs and selectable options accessible.

Privacy:
- Do not store submitted messages in local storage or a database.
- Keep analysis in memory during the current session.
- Include a clear privacy note encouraging users not to paste identifying or highly sensitive information.
- Make clear that the current prototype uses local demonstration logic and does not connect to a real AI service.
- Do not claim guaranteed privacy or confidentiality.
